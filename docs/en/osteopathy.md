## Where osteopathy comes from

Andrew Taylor Still, a Union army physician turned practitioner in Missouri, set out osteopathy in 1874, after losing three of his children to meningitis and judging the medicine of his day unable to help them. He opened his school in Kirksville in 1892.

His governing idea is not mystical and it fits in a sentence: go back to the cause rather than stop at the effect. He wrote it himself, in 1910, in *Osteopathy: Research and Practice*.

> He should never dally with effects but ever go back to the cause which when corrected results in a disappearance of the effect.

The sentence is on page 10 of the book, [digitised by Harvard’s medical library](https://wellcomecollection.org/works/ypa64cm4) and out of copyright, if you want to read it in context.

That logic is what the long conversation at the start of a session is for, described on the [about](/en/about/) page. It is also why an osteopath takes an interest in a fall fifteen years ago.

## Osteopath in Québec, D.O. in the United States

This is the most common confusion, and it comes from English-language sources.

In the United States, a *Doctor of Osteopathic Medicine*, abbreviated D.O., is a full physician. Four years of medical school, a residency, prescribing rights, surgery, hospital practice, exactly as an M.D. does. The two degrees are equivalent under American law.

In Québec, an osteopath is a manual therapist. No medical licence, no prescribing, no medical diagnosis, no practice inside the public system. The word is the same, the role is not.

So when a search in English brings back impressive material about osteopathy in hospital medicine, it is not describing what I do.

## Where the profession stands in Québec

Osteopathy is not yet a regulated profession here. There is no professional order, no reserved title and no compulsory university programme. Anyone may currently advertise themselves as an osteopath.

That is changing. On 25 March 2026 the government announced that osteopathy would become the province’s 57th regulated profession. On 16 June it specified that osteopaths would be integrated into the [Ordre des chiropraticiens du Québec](https://www.ordredeschiropraticiens.ca/) rather than forming a separate order. The transition rules, the timeline and the fate of the current titles are still to be determined.

Until then, what governs me is RITMA membership: a code of ethics, admission criteria, professional liability insurance and a complaints process. That is less than a professional order, and I would rather write it than let you assume otherwise.

## What the research shows

Three things, in order of strength.

**For chronic low back pain the evidence is the most developed.** A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis pooled seven trials and about 378 participants. It reports a reduction in pain of 1.3 points on a 10-point scale against sham or no treatment, with a level of certainty its authors call moderate, and no serious adverse event attributed. They conclude the approach holds up as a low-risk adjunct. Three limits, which they name themselves: the effect is short-term, six weeks at most, the sample is small, and 1.3 points out of 10 is a real but modest improvement. [Reference](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42136223/).

**For fibromyalgia, the reference guideline does not put me first.** The European recommendation published in 2017 by EULAR retains only one approach with a strong recommendation, adapted physical exercise. All the others, manual approaches included, are graded more weakly. [Reference](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27377815/).

**One technique is an exception, in a precise sense.** A 2021 systematic review grants myofascial release a moderate level of evidence on pain, sleep and quality of life in fibromyalgia, across six studies and 279 participants, compared with sham or no treatment. Its authors add that trials comparing the approach with other manual care are missing: nobody has shown it does better than another pair of hands. [Reference](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34507243/).

What I take from that, and what I will tell you in session if you ask: osteopathy has a place as an adjunct, at low risk, with measured effects that are real and modest. Not as a first line, not as a substitute, and not with certainties the literature does not contain.
